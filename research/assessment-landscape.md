## TL;DR

Clinical trial complexity assessment tools include OPAL, the Protocol Complexity Tool (PCT), TRACAT (with trial rating indicators), Pharm‑CAT, and several public feasibility checklists; they evaluate domains such as operational burden, regulatory oversight, patient and site burden, recruitment, and resources. Validations and use cases vary; many checklists lack robust user testing.

----

## Existing assessment tools

Clinical sites and sponsors use several named tools and publicly available checklists to rate protocol complexity and inform accept/participate decisions. Below is a concise comparison of commonly reported instruments and checklist families.

| Tool or checklist | Scope and purpose | Structure and key elements | Validation or evidence of use |
|---|---:|---|---|
| **OPAL** | Site workload and staffing planning for oncology trials | Protocol‑level rating scale to quantify study workload | Pilot across 17 Ontario centres reviewing 27 protocols demonstrated score consistency across sites [1]. |
| **Protocol Complexity Tool (PCT)** | Drive protocol simplification and estimate total complexity | 26 questions across 5 domains: operational execution, regulatory oversight, patient burden, site burden, study design [2] | Applied to difficult phase II–IV trials; total complexity score correlated with activation and recruitment metrics [2]. |
| **TRACAT / TRIs** | Cancer trial follow‑up and delivery complexity from multidisciplinary consensus | Delphi‑derived statements and ranked trial rating indicators (TRIs) spanning follow‑up intensity, operational performance, and organisational factors [3] | Consensus eDelphi produced 75 statements and 14 ranked TRIs from research professionals [3]. |
| **Pharm‑CAT** | Pharmacy workload for sponsored oncology/haematology trials | 15 items in three sections yielding an ordinal complexity score with tertile cutoffs [4] | Applied to 60 trials; interrater scoring and predefined low/medium/high cutoffs reported [4]. |
| **Public feasibility checklists** | Broad feasibility assessment tools used by investigators, funders, ethics boards | Collections of items covering regulation, recruitment, space/equipment, finances, team resources, trial management, pilot studies [5] | Scoping review found 10 publicly available checklists but reported none appeared user‑tested or validated [5]. |

Each tool targets slightly different decision needs (workload/staffing, pharmacy burden, protocol simplification, or general feasibility) and therefore captures overlapping but not identical factors [1] [2] [3] [4] [5].

## Key complexity dimensions

The literature converges on a core set of protocol and operational domains that drive complexity and the accept/participate decision. The following dimensions recur across tools and reviews.

- **Operational execution and site burden**  
  Protocol activities, visit schedules, monitoring frequency, and site tasks that increase staff time and coordination needs are central in several tools [2] [1].  
- **Patient burden and recruitment**  
  Number and invasiveness of assessments, participant follow‑up intensity, and recruitment difficulty affect enrollments and retention [2] [3] [5].  
- **Regulatory and oversight requirements**  
  Complexity due to safety reporting, special approvals, or additional oversight steps is explicitly scored in multiple frameworks [2] [5].  
- **Resources and finances**  
  Availability of space, equipment, investigational product handling, and budget adequacy influence feasibility and staffing decisions [5] [4].  
- **Trial design complexity**  
  Adaptive elements, multiple cohorts/endpoints, complex eligibility criteria and frequent protocol amendments raise execution risk [2] [6].  
- **Team and organisational factors**  
  Local expertise, communication, interoperability, and organisational capacity were highlighted as drivers of delivery difficulty in consensus work [3].  
- **Function‑specific burdens**  
  Examples include pharmacy handling complexity (investigational drug storage, accountability) captured in pharmacy‑specific tools [4].

The scoping review synthesized 48 distinct items and grouped them into seven feasibility domains broadly aligning with the dimensions above, reflecting common content across publicly available checklists [5].

## Tool validation evidence

Decisionmakers should weigh available validation and practical testing when choosing an assessment instrument. Evidence quality and breadth differ across tools.

- **OPAL** demonstrated intersite consistency during a multi‑site pilot, with most site OPAL score differences between 0 and 1.5 when 27 protocols were reviewed, supporting reliability for workload planning in oncology [1]. A subsequent external validation effort has been reported in a separate setting [7].  
- **PCT** showed responsiveness: total complexity scores decreased after tool‑guided protocol review in 75% of assessed trials, and TCS correlated with time‑to‑site activation and participant enrollment in the tested sample [2].  
- **TRACAT / TRIs** were produced by a structured eDelphi with multidisciplinary clinical research professionals, yielding consensus statements and ranked indicators to inform trial rating and complexity assessment in cancer trials [3].  
- **Pharm‑CAT** reported user‑friendliness, interrater application, and empiric cutoffs (low/medium/high) from 60 evaluated trials, but authors recommended prospective multicentre validation [4].  
- **Public checklists** are numerous but, per the scoping review, generally lack user testing and formal validation, limiting evidence for predictive performance [5].

Where validation is limited, local piloting and comparison against past site performance metrics are advisable before relying on a tool for go/no‑go decisions [5] [2].

## Practical use considerations

Selecting or implementing a complexity assessment should match the decision context and available evidence; the literature offers several practical lessons.  

- **Match tool to purpose**  
  Use workforce/staffing tools (OPAL, Pharm‑CAT) when the key decision concerns local capacity, use PCT or TRACAT when protocol simplification or delivery complexity is the objective, and use general feasibility checklists for broader go/no‑go screening [1] [4] [2] [3] [5].  
- **Pilot and adapt locally**  
  Given variable validation and site contexts, pilot a chosen instrument against historical trials and adjust scoring thresholds to local operations and staffing models [5] [4].  
- **Link scores to measurable actions**  
  Convert complexity scores into staffing models, budget adjustments, or contractual clarifications (e.g., third‑party responsibilities) to make the assessment actionable [1] [8] [9].  
- **Combine quantitative scoring with qualitative judgement**  
  Delphi‑style consensus outputs and organisational readiness items matter for accept/participate decisions and should complement numeric scores [3].  
- **Monitor predictive performance**  
  Track whether tool scores predict activation timelines, recruitment, or resource shortfalls; use those data to refine thresholds and escalation rules [2] [1].

If users require further synthesis or a tailored recommendation for a specific site or trial type, insufficient evidence prevents prescribing a single universally superior tool; local piloting and alignment with the decision objective are recommended [5].

**Applied example:** This project's own checklist (`checklist-report.md`) followed exactly this "pilot and adapt locally" pattern for a non-malignant hematology (sickle cell disease, hemophilia, bone marrow failure, bleeding/clotting disorders) CTM caseload — see `hem-ctm-checklist-addendum-draft.md` for the specific items added (event-driven visit burden, long-term follow-up commitment, a supplemental data/specimen-complexity module) and the rationale for keeping some of that customization out of the core weighted score entirely rather than forcing every study design onto one composite number.
