# Clinical Trial Complexity Assessment Checklist for Go/No-Go Decision-Making

## Executive Summary

This report presents a structured complexity assessment checklist designed to support clinical trial sites and organizations in making informed go/no-go decisions when evaluating whether to participate in a clinical trial. The framework synthesizes evidence from validated complexity assessment tools including the Ontario Protocol Assessment Level (OPAL), the Protocol Complexity Tool (PCT), the Trial Rating and Complexity Assessment Tool (TRACAT), and Pharm-CAT, alongside publicly available feasibility checklists.

The checklist organizes complexity assessment across five core dimensions: protocol design and study complexity, operational execution and site burden, regulatory and oversight requirements, resource and workforce capacity, and patient population and recruitment feasibility. Each dimension includes scored assessment items with clear guidance for interpretation. A weighted scoring system enables sites to calculate an overall complexity score, which is then mapped to decision thresholds (low, medium, high complexity) that inform go/no-go recommendations. A supplemental sixth dimension (§8a) scores study-design modality and data/specimen complexity — qualitative methods, EMR abstraction, biospecimens, PROs, and longitudinal cohort scale — for non-interventional designs (natural history studies, registries, qualitative research); it is reported as its own number and is not blended into the core 0-100 score.

This evidence-based framework addresses a critical gap in clinical trial planning, as approximately one in four clinical trials fail to complete as planned, primarily due to inadequate feasibility assessment and poor recruitment [1], [3], [5], [13], [18]. By systematically evaluating trial complexity before commitment, sites can better align trial requirements with available capacity, reduce startup delays, optimize resource allocation, and improve trial success rates.

## 1. Introduction

Clinical trial site selection and participation decisions represent critical junctures that significantly impact trial success, resource utilization, and institutional capacity. Sites face increasing pressure to evaluate trial opportunities rapidly while ensuring alignment between protocol requirements and available capabilities. However, many sites lack structured frameworks for systematically assessing trial complexity and feasibility, leading to overcommitment, resource strain, delayed activations, and poor enrollment performance [14], [15], [19].

The complexity of clinical trials has increased substantially over the past decade, driven by more precise inclusion and exclusion criteria, narrow enrollment windows, intensive monitoring requirements, biomarker assessments, and expanding regulatory obligations [3], [9], [11]. This escalating complexity contributes to delays in trial implementation, execution, recruitment, and data management, ultimately increasing costs and extending timelines [3], [12].

This report addresses these challenges by presenting a comprehensive, evidence-based complexity assessment checklist specifically designed for go/no-go decision-making at the site level. The framework draws upon validated tools and published literature to provide sites with a practical, systematic approach to evaluating trial opportunities. By quantifying complexity across multiple dimensions and linking scores to actionable decision thresholds, this checklist enables sites to make informed participation decisions that balance scientific opportunity with operational reality.

## 2. Background and Theoretical Foundations

### 2.1 Existing Complexity Assessment Tools

Several validated tools have been developed to assess clinical trial complexity and inform feasibility decisions. The most prominent frameworks include:

**Ontario Protocol Assessment Level (OPAL):** Developed for oncology trials, OPAL uses a pyramid rating scale ranging from level 1 (less complex, non-treatment trials) to level 8 (complex phase I intervention trials), with optional elements adding 0.5 increments for a maximum score of 10 [5]. The tool quantifies trial complexity based on core protocol requirements and incremental procedures, with scores directly informing workload estimation and staffing decisions [1], [5]. OPAL has demonstrated inter-site consistency, with most site score differences between 0 and 1.5 when 27 protocols were reviewed across 17 Ontario centers [5].

**Protocol Complexity Tool (PCT):** This framework comprises 26 questions across five domains: operational execution, regulatory oversight, patient burden, site burden, and study design [3]. Individual domain scores combine to produce a total complexity score (TCS) that has been shown to correlate significantly with time-to-site activation (rho = 0.61, p = 0.005 at 75% activation) and participant enrollment (rho = 0.59, p = 0.012 at 25% recruitment) [3]. In 75% of assessed trials, protocol review using the PCT resulted in reduced complexity scores, demonstrating the tool's utility in driving protocol simplification [3].

**Trial Rating and Complexity Assessment Tool (TRACAT):** Developed through an eDelphi consensus process with research professionals, TRACAT incorporates 75 consensus statements and 14 ranked Trial Rating Indicators (TRIs) that address complexity, follow-up intensity, and operational performance in cancer trial delivery [4]. The tool provides a mechanism for continual knowledge assimilation and supports multidisciplinary assessment of trial feasibility [4].

**Pharmacy Complexity Assessment Tool (Pharm-CAT):** Designed specifically for pharmacy workload assessment in oncology and hematology trials, Pharm-CAT uses 15 items across three sections to generate a total complexity score [13]. Scores are categorized into ordinal levels: low (0-19), medium (20-25), and high (≥26), with cutoffs corresponding to tertiles of the score distribution [13].

### 2.2 Core Complexity Dimensions

Analysis of these validated tools and feasibility checklists reveals convergence on five core complexity dimensions that drive site burden and influence trial success:

1. **Protocol Design and Study Complexity:** Trial phase, adaptive elements, multiple cohorts or endpoints, complex eligibility criteria, and protocol amendment frequency [3], [5], [7], [8], [9]
2. **Operational Execution and Site Burden:** Visit schedules, monitoring frequency, assessment procedures, data collection requirements, and coordination needs [3], [5], [11], [18]
3. **Regulatory and Oversight Requirements:** Safety reporting obligations, special approvals, ethics review complexity, and regulatory submission requirements [3], [9], [14], [17], [18]
4. **Resource and Workforce Capacity:** Staffing availability, specialized expertise, space and equipment, investigational product handling, and budget adequacy [5], [10], [13], [15], [18]
5. **Patient Population and Recruitment:** Target population accessibility, recruitment feasibility, enrollment timelines, retention challenges, and patient burden [3], [4], [6], [15], [18]

A scoping review of publicly available feasibility checklists identified 48 distinct assessment items organized into seven domains that align closely with these core dimensions: regulation/review/oversight, participant recruitment, space/material/equipment, financial resources, trial team resources, trial management, and pilot/feasibility studies [18]. However, the review found that none of the available checklists appeared to be user-tested or validated, highlighting the need for evidence-based assessment frameworks [18].

### 2.3 Rationale for Structured Assessment

Structured complexity assessment serves multiple critical functions in trial planning and site selection. First, it enables objective quantification of trial requirements, facilitating comparison across opportunities and alignment with site capacity [1], [5]. Second, complexity scores correlate with key performance indicators including activation timelines and enrollment rates, providing predictive value for trial success [3]. Third, systematic assessment identifies specific areas of concern that can be addressed through protocol modification, resource allocation, or risk mitigation strategies [3], [11].

Evidence demonstrates that centralized feasibility management using structured assessment processes can improve efficiency and decision quality. One study of 938 feasibility requests across 30 clinical trial units found that 53% of assessments resulted in accepted offers, with declined offers most commonly attributed to insufficient patient volume (31%), competing studies (17.4%), unacceptable study design (13.9%), and inadequate resources (9.9%) [19]. Median processing time was six days, demonstrating that structured processes can support rapid yet thorough evaluation [19].

## 3. Complexity Assessment Framework

### 3.1 Framework Structure

The complexity assessment checklist is organized into five dimensions, each containing multiple scored assessment items. Sites evaluate each item using a standardized scoring scale and calculate dimension-specific subtotals and an overall complexity score. The framework incorporates:

- **Dimension-specific checklists** with 6-10 items per dimension
- **Standardized scoring scales** (0-3 points per item, with defined anchors)
- **Weighted dimension scores** reflecting relative impact on site burden
- **Total complexity score** (0-100 scale) for overall assessment
- **Decision thresholds** mapping scores to go/no-go recommendations

### 3.2 Scoring Principles

The scoring system is designed to be:

- **Objective:** Items are defined with clear, observable criteria to minimize subjective interpretation
- **Granular:** Four-point scales (0-3) provide sufficient discrimination without excessive complexity
- **Weighted:** Dimension weights reflect empirical evidence of impact on trial success and site burden
- **Actionable:** Scores map directly to decision categories and resource planning needs

### 3.3 Assessment Process

Sites should complete the assessment following this process:

1. **Gather protocol information:** Obtain complete protocol, informed consent forms, schedule of assessments, budget, and sponsor requirements
2. **Assemble assessment team:** Include principal investigator, research coordinators, regulatory staff, pharmacy (if applicable), and administrative leadership
3. **Score each dimension:** Evaluate all items within each dimension using the provided scoring guidance
4. **Calculate dimension subtotals:** Sum item scores within each dimension
5. **Apply dimension weights:** Multiply dimension subtotals by assigned weights
6. **Calculate total complexity score:** Sum weighted dimension scores
7. **Interpret results:** Map total score to decision threshold and review dimension-specific findings
8. **Make go/no-go decision:** Consider total score, dimension profiles, and institutional priorities

## 4. Dimension 1: Protocol Design and Study Complexity

### 4.1 Rationale

Protocol design fundamentally determines trial complexity, influencing all downstream operational, regulatory, and resource requirements. More complex designs—including early-phase trials, adaptive protocols, multiple treatment arms, and frequent amendments—impose greater burdens on sites and correlate with longer activation times and enrollment challenges [3], [5], [9]. The OPAL framework explicitly ranks trial complexity from level 1 (non-treatment observational studies) to level 8 (complex phase I intervention trials), demonstrating the foundational role of protocol design in complexity assessment [5].

### 4.3 Interpretation Guidance

- **Low complexity (0-7):** Standard protocol design with straightforward implementation requirements

High scores in this dimension indicate protocols that will demand substantial investigator expertise, specialized infrastructure, and careful coordination. Sites should assess whether they possess the necessary therapeutic area experience, biomarker testing capabilities, and adaptive trial management skills before proceeding [3], [5], [9].

## 5. Dimension 2: Operational Execution and Site Burden

### 5.1 Rationale

Operational execution requirements directly determine site workload, coordinator time, and resource consumption. Visit frequency, assessment procedures, monitoring intensity, and data collection demands are primary drivers of site burden and have been shown to correlate with activation timelines and enrollment performance [3], [11]. The PCT framework identifies operational execution as one of five core domains, with complexity in this dimension frequently reduced through protocol simplification efforts [3]. Schedule of assessments (SoA) complexity has been specifically identified as a major source of burden on participants, sites, and sponsors, affecting recruitment, retention, costs, and cycle times [11].

### 5.3 Interpretation Guidance

- **Low complexity (0-9):** Manageable operational requirements within standard site workflows
- **Medium complexity (10-18):** Moderate operational burden requiring dedicated coordinator time and coordination
- **High complexity (19-27):** Intensive operational requirements demanding significant site resources and specialized capabilities

Operational execution represents the largest component of site burden and is weighted most heavily in the overall complexity score. High scores indicate protocols that will consume substantial coordinator time, require careful scheduling, and may strain site capacity. Sites should carefully assess whether current workload and staffing levels can accommodate the operational demands [3], [10], [11].

## 6. Dimension 3: Regulatory and Oversight Requirements

### 6.1 Rationale

Regulatory and oversight requirements have increased substantially in recent years, driven by safety concerns, legal requirements for data management and informed consent, and specific therapeutic area needs [9], [14], [17]. Regulatory complexity contributes to startup delays, with regulatory approval processes identified as a key driver of delays in global randomized trials [14]. The PCT framework explicitly includes regulatory oversight as a core complexity domain, and feasibility checklists consistently incorporate regulation, review, and oversight as a distinct assessment area [3], [18].

## 7. Dimension 4: Resource and Workforce Capacity

### 7.1 Rationale

Resource and workforce capacity represents a fundamental constraint on trial participation. Inadequate resources are a leading cause of declined trial opportunities, accounting for 9.9% of declined feasibility assessments in one large study [19]. The OPAL framework explicitly links complexity scores to workload estimation and staffing decisions, recognizing that protocol complexity must be balanced against available capacity [1], [5]. Workforce planning tools assess how protocol complexity affects clinical research coordinator productivity and cumulative workload over time [10]. Feasibility checklists consistently include assessment of space, material, equipment, financial resources, and trial team resources as core domains [18].

## 8. Dimension 5: Patient Population and Recruitment

### 8.1 Rationale

Patient recruitment is the most common cause of trial failure, with poor recruitment accounting for the majority of trials that do not complete as planned [16], [18]. Recruitment feasibility depends on multiple factors including target population accessibility, enrollment timelines, patient burden, and competing studies [15], [19]. The PCT framework identifies patient burden as a core complexity domain, recognizing that excessive burden affects recruitment and retention [3]. Feasibility checklists consistently include participant recruitment as a distinct assessment domain [18]. One study found that insufficient expected patient volume was the most common reason for declining trial opportunities, accounting for 31% of declined offers [19].

### 8.2 Assessment Items

| Item | Description | Scoring Guidance | Score (0-3) |
|------|-------------|------------------|-------------|
| **5.1 Target Population Accessibility** | Availability of eligible patients at site | 0 = Large eligible population readily accessible<br>1 = Moderate eligible population accessible<br>2 = Small eligible population, requires active recruitment<br>3 = Very limited eligible population or rare disease | _____ |

| **5.4 Patient Burden** | Time, travel, and procedure burden on participants | 0 = Minimal burden, convenient for patients<br>1 = Moderate burden, manageable for most patients<br>2 = Substantial burden, may deter some patients<br>3 = Excessive burden likely to significantly impact recruitment and retention | _____ |
| **5.5 Retention Challenges** | Anticipated difficulty retaining participants | 0 = Low dropout risk, short duration<br>1 = Moderate retention expected<br>2 = Retention challenges anticipated<br>3 = High dropout risk due to duration, burden, or population characteristics | _____ |
| **5.6 Recruitment Strategy Feasibility** | Viability of proposed recruitment methods | 0 = Proven recruitment methods, high confidence<br>1 = Standard methods, moderate confidence<br>2 = Novel methods or uncertain effectiveness<br>3 = Recruitment strategy unclear or likely ineffective | _____ |

**Dimension 5 Subtotal:** _____ / 18

**Dimension 5 Weighted Score:** Subtotal × 1.0 = _____ / 18

### 8.3 Interpretation Guidance

- **Low complexity (0-6):** Favorable recruitment conditions with accessible population and manageable burden
- **Medium complexity (7-12):** Moderate recruitment challenges requiring active recruitment strategies
- **High complexity (13-18):** Substantial recruitment barriers likely to impact enrollment timelines and success

Recruitment feasibility is the single most important predictor of trial success. High scores indicate trials with significant recruitment risk that may fail to meet enrollment targets. Sites should carefully assess whether the eligible patient population, recruitment timeline, and patient burden align with realistic enrollment projections. Competing studies and excessive patient burden are particularly important red flags [3], [15], [16], [18], [19].

> **Note for rare/ultra-rare caseloads:** For sites whose caseload is predominantly rare/ultra-rare conditions, the bands above will systematically skew Medium–High, since a small eligible population (5.1) and competing sponsor trials for it (5.3) are the norm rather than the exception in this space. This should not by itself be read as elevated risk. Per §11.1 Phase 2 (Calibration): after your pilot cohort, compute the site's own historical Dimension 5 score distribution and set Low/Medium/High bands from your own quartiles rather than the generic thresholds above — this keeps Dimension 5 useful for differentiating your typical caseload instead of flattening everything to "high."

## 8a. Supplemental Dimension 6: Study Design Modality & Data/Specimen Complexity

**Kept separate from the core 0–100 complexity score and Go/No-Go decision.** A large longitudinal EMR-abstraction cohort study and a Phase II drug trial aren't complex in comparable ways — forcing both onto one composite would either dilute the drug-trial signal or produce a misleadingly low score for a cohort study that has no treatment arms or IP handling but is enormous in scope. Report this dimension as its own parallel readout: *"Core Complexity: XX/100 (GO/CONDITIONAL/NO-GO)"* alongside *"Data & Specimen Complexity: XX/21 (Low/Medium/High)."*

**Applicability note for Item 1.1 (Trial Phase):** its "0 = Phase IV or observational" anchor should not be read as "observational = simple." A non-interventional study will legitimately score low/not-applicable on several Dimension 1–4 items (treatment arms, IP handling) without that indicating true simplicity — this dimension is where that study's actual complexity should surface.

### 8a.1 Rationale

Qualitative research and large longitudinal cohort studies built on retrospective and prospective EMR abstraction, biospecimen collection, and PRO batteries are common in non-malignant hematology (natural history studies, registries, patient-experience research) but don't fit an interventional-trial-shaped checklist. This dimension gives them the same structured, comparable scoring the other five give drug/device trials.

**Dimension 6 Subtotal:** _____ / 21 *(not weighted, not added to the core Total Complexity Score)*

### 8a.3 Interpretation Guidance

- **Low (0-7):** Straightforward data/specimen scope manageable within standard research-coordinator workflows.
- **Medium (8-14):** Meaningful data-management or specimen-handling burden; likely needs dedicated data-abstraction or informatics support.
- **High (15-21):** Substantial standing infrastructure required — informatics/data-management staff, biobanking capability, and/or qualitative-methods expertise — comparable in planning weight to a high-complexity interventional trial, even though it may score low on Dimensions 1-4.

## 9. Scoring Guidance and Interpretation

### 9.1 Calculating the Total Complexity Score

The total complexity score is calculated by summing the weighted dimension scores:

**Total Complexity Score = (Dimension 1 × 1.2) + (Dimension 2 × 1.5) + (Dimension 3 × 1.0) + (Dimension 4 × 1.3) + (Dimension 5 × 1.0)**

*(Dimension 6, if used, is scored and reported separately and is not part of this formula — see §8a.)*

**Maximum Possible Score:** 132.9 *(25.2 + 40.5 + 18 + 31.2 + 18, reflecting Items 2.9 and 4.8)*

**Normalized Score (0-100 scale):** (Total Complexity Score / 132.9) × 100 = _____

### 9.2 Dimension Weighting Rationale

Dimension weights reflect empirical evidence of impact on trial success and site burden:

- **Dimension 1 (Protocol Design): 1.2** – Foundational complexity that influences all other dimensions; OPAL demonstrates strong correlation between protocol complexity level and workload [5]
- **Dimension 2 (Operational Execution): 1.5** – Highest weight reflecting direct impact on site burden; PCT shows operational execution is the most frequently simplified domain and correlates with activation and enrollment timelines [3]
- **Dimension 3 (Regulatory): 1.0** – Standard weight; regulatory requirements are important but typically manageable within existing processes for most trials
- **Dimension 4 (Resources): 1.3** – High weight reflecting resource constraints as a primary reason for declining trials [19]
- **Dimension 5 (Recruitment): 1.0** – Standard weight; while recruitment is critical for success, this dimension assesses feasibility rather than burden

### 9.3 Complexity Categories

Total complexity scores map to three categories:

| Category | Normalized Score Range | Interpretation |
|----------|------------------------|----------------|
| **Low Complexity** | 0-40 | Trial requirements align well with standard site capabilities. Minimal additional resources or specialized expertise required. Low risk of operational challenges. |
| **Medium Complexity** | 41-65 | Trial presents moderate challenges requiring careful planning, dedicated resources, or specialized expertise. Manageable with appropriate preparation and resource allocation. |
| **High Complexity** | 66-100 | Trial demands substantial resources, specialized capabilities, or intensive operational support. High risk of operational challenges, resource strain, or enrollment difficulties. Requires exceptional justification and mitigation strategies. |

### 9.4 Dimension Profile Analysis

In addition to the total complexity score, sites should analyze the dimension profile to identify specific areas of concern:

- **Dimension scores >70% of maximum** indicate areas requiring focused attention and mitigation strategies
- **Multiple dimensions >70%** suggest cumulative burden that may exceed site capacity
- **Dimension 5 (Recruitment) >70%** is a critical red flag given recruitment's role in trial failure

### 9.5 Score Interpretation Examples

**Example 1: Low Complexity Trial**
- Dimension 1: 5/21 (24%) – Phase III, simple design
- Dimension 2: 8/27 (30%) – Moderate visit schedule, Item 2.9 = 0
- Dimension 3: 4/18 (22%) – Standard regulatory requirements
- Dimension 4: 6/24 (25%) – Adequate resources available, Item 4.8 = 0
- Dimension 5: 5/18 (28%) – Good recruitment feasibility
- **Total Score: 26/100** – Low complexity, favorable for participation

**Example 2: High Complexity Trial**
- Dimension 1: 16/21 (76%) – Phase I, adaptive design, biomarkers
- Dimension 2: 20/27 (74%) – Intensive visits, complex procedures
- Dimension 3: 12/18 (67%) – DSMB, complex consent
- Dimension 4: 18/24 (75%) – Specialized expertise, limited capacity
- Dimension 5: 14/18 (78%) – Rare disease, aggressive timeline
- **Total Score: 74/100** – High complexity, requires exceptional justification

## 10. Decision Matrix and Go/No-Go Thresholds

### 10.1 Primary Decision Framework

The decision matrix integrates total complexity score with dimension-specific thresholds to generate go/no-go recommendations:

| Total Complexity Score | Dimension 5 (Recruitment) | Primary Recommendation | Conditions |
|------------------------|---------------------------|------------------------|------------|
| **0-40 (Low)** | 0-6 (Low) | **GO** | Proceed with standard activation process |
| **0-40 (Low)** | 7-12 (Medium) | **GO with Monitoring** | Proceed with enhanced recruitment planning |
| **0-40 (Low)** | 13-18 (High) | **CONDITIONAL GO** | Proceed only with robust recruitment mitigation plan |
| **41-65 (Medium)** | 0-6 (Low) | **GO with Planning** | Proceed with resource allocation and risk mitigation plan |
| **41-65 (Medium)** | 7-12 (Medium) | **CONDITIONAL GO** | Proceed only if adequate resources and recruitment strategy confirmed |
| **41-65 (Medium)** | 13-18 (High) | **NO-GO** | Decline unless exceptional strategic value and mitigation plan |
| **66-100 (High)** | 0-6 (Low) | **CONDITIONAL GO** | Proceed only with exceptional justification and comprehensive mitigation plan |
| **66-100 (High)** | 7-12 (Medium) | **NO-GO** | Decline unless transformational opportunity with full institutional support |
| **66-100 (High)** | 13-18 (High) | **NO-GO** | Decline; risk of failure too high |

### 10.2 Secondary Decision Factors

In addition to the primary decision framework, sites should consider:

**Strategic Alignment:**
- Does the trial align with institutional research priorities and therapeutic area focus?
- Does participation support strategic relationships with sponsors or networks?
- Does the trial offer unique scientific or educational value?

**Resource Availability:**
- Can required resources be secured without compromising existing commitments?
- Is the budget adequate to cover all anticipated costs plus contingency?
- Are specialized expertise and infrastructure available or obtainable?

**Risk Tolerance:**
- What is the site's current portfolio risk profile?
- Can the site absorb potential enrollment shortfalls or operational challenges?
- Are there contractual or reputational risks associated with potential underperformance?

**Mitigation Feasibility:**
- Can identified high-complexity areas be mitigated through protocol negotiation, additional resources, or operational strategies?
- Is the sponsor willing to collaborate on complexity reduction or enhanced support?
- Are there successful precedents for managing similar complexity at the site?

### 10.3 Conditional GO Requirements

Trials receiving a "Conditional GO" recommendation should proceed only if the following requirements are met:

1. **Documented Mitigation Plan:** Written plan addressing each dimension scoring >70% of maximum, with specific mitigation strategies, responsible parties, and timelines
2. **Resource Commitment:** Confirmed availability of required resources, including budget approval, staffing plan, and infrastructure access
3. **Leadership Approval:** Explicit approval from institutional leadership acknowledging complexity and resource requirements
4. **Sponsor Collaboration:** Sponsor agreement to support mitigation strategies (e.g., protocol modifications, enhanced monitoring support, recruitment assistance)
5. **Performance Monitoring:** Defined metrics and review schedule to monitor trial performance and trigger escalation if needed

### 10.4 NO-GO Decision Guidance

Trials receiving a "NO-GO" recommendation should be declined unless:

- The trial represents a transformational scientific opportunity aligned with institutional strategic priorities
- Full institutional leadership support is secured, including commitment of exceptional resources
- A comprehensive mitigation plan addresses all high-complexity dimensions with realistic strategies
- The sponsor demonstrates willingness to collaborate on complexity reduction and enhanced support
- The site has demonstrated success with similarly complex trials in the past

Even with these conditions, sites should carefully consider whether accepting a high-complexity trial would compromise existing commitments or create unacceptable risk of failure.

### 10.5 Decision Documentation

All go/no-go decisions should be documented with:

- Completed complexity assessment checklist with scores
- Total complexity score and dimension profile
- Decision recommendation and rationale
- Mitigation plan (for Conditional GO decisions)
- Leadership approval (for Conditional GO and exceptional NO-GO overrides)
- Date and decision-makers involved

This documentation supports institutional learning, enables retrospective analysis of decision quality, and provides accountability for resource allocation decisions.

## 11. Implementation Recommendations

### 11.1 Adoption and Validation

Sites implementing this complexity assessment checklist should follow a phased approach:

**Phase 1: Pilot Testing (3-6 months)**
- Apply the checklist to 5-10 recent trial opportunities (both accepted and declined)
- Compare complexity scores to actual trial performance (activation time, enrollment rate, resource consumption)
- Gather feedback from assessment team members on usability and relevance
- Identify any site-specific factors requiring additional assessment items

**Phase 2: Calibration (6-12 months)**
- Adjust scoring anchors and dimension weights based on pilot data and site-specific experience
- Establish site-specific decision thresholds based on historical performance and risk tolerance
- Validate that complexity scores correlate with key performance indicators (activation time, enrollment success, resource utilization)
- Refine the assessment process based on user feedback

**Phase 3: Full Implementation (ongoing)**
- Integrate the checklist into standard feasibility assessment workflow
- Train all relevant staff on proper use and interpretation
- Monitor predictive performance and refine as needed
- Conduct annual review of scoring criteria and decision thresholds

This phased approach aligns with recommendations from the literature emphasizing the importance of local piloting and adaptation given variable validation and site contexts [18].

### 11.2 Integration with Existing Processes

The complexity assessment checklist should be integrated into existing feasibility and site selection workflows:

**Timing:** Complete the assessment during the feasibility phase, after receiving the protocol but before committing to participation

**Responsibility:** Assign a feasibility coordinator or research manager to coordinate the assessment, with input from:
- Principal investigator (protocol design, scientific merit)
- Research coordinators (operational execution, patient burden)
- Regulatory affairs (regulatory requirements)
- Pharmacy (investigational product handling, if applicable)
- Finance/administration (budget, resources, capacity)
- Recruitment coordinator (patient population, recruitment feasibility)

**Integration Points:**
- Use complexity score to inform feasibility questionnaire responses to sponsors
- Include complexity assessment in internal feasibility review meetings
- Link complexity scores to resource allocation and staffing decisions
- Incorporate complexity data into portfolio management and capacity planning

### 11.3 Customization Considerations

Sites should consider customizing the checklist to reflect:

**Therapeutic Area Specialization:** Add or modify items relevant to specific disease areas (e.g., oncology-specific items for cancer centers, pediatric considerations for children's hospitals)

**Institutional Priorities:** Adjust dimension weights to reflect institutional strategic priorities (e.g., higher weight for scientific innovation at academic medical centers)

**Resource Constraints:** Modify scoring anchors to reflect site-specific resource availability and capacity

**Regulatory Environment:** Adapt regulatory items to reflect local regulatory requirements and institutional review processes

**Portfolio Characteristics:** Consider current portfolio composition when assessing capacity and competing studies

However, sites should maintain core structure and scoring principles to enable comparison across trials and learning from historical data.

### 11.4 Training and Quality Assurance

Effective implementation requires:

**Initial Training:**
- Conduct training sessions for all staff involved in feasibility assessment
- Provide scoring guidance and examples for each assessment item
- Practice scoring with historical protocols to calibrate understanding
- Establish inter-rater reliability through parallel scoring exercises

**Ongoing Quality Assurance:**
- Conduct periodic inter-rater reliability checks
- Review scoring consistency across different assessment teams
- Validate that scores correlate with actual trial performance
- Update training materials based on lessons learned

**Documentation:**
- Maintain a scoring manual with detailed guidance for each item
- Document site-specific customizations and rationale
- Preserve completed assessments for retrospective analysis
- Track decision outcomes and trial performance

### 11.5 Continuous Improvement

Sites should treat the complexity assessment checklist as a living tool that evolves based on experience:

**Performance Monitoring:**
- Track correlation between complexity scores and trial outcomes (activation time, enrollment success, resource consumption, protocol deviations)
- Identify dimensions or items with strongest predictive value
- Analyze trials where complexity assessment did not accurately predict performance

**Refinement:**
- Adjust scoring criteria based on performance data
- Modify dimension weights to improve predictive accuracy
- Add or remove items based on relevance and predictive value
- Update decision thresholds based on institutional risk tolerance and capacity

**Knowledge Sharing:**
- Share lessons learned across the institution
- Contribute to broader literature on complexity assessment and feasibility
- Participate in multi-site validation efforts when available
- Benchmark against other sites using similar frameworks

This continuous improvement approach aligns with recommendations to monitor predictive performance and use data to refine thresholds and escalation rules [1], [2], [3].

## 12. Conclusion

This complexity assessment checklist provides clinical trial sites and organizations with a structured, evidence-based framework for evaluating trial opportunities and making informed go/no-go decisions. By systematically assessing complexity across five core dimensions—protocol design, operational execution, regulatory requirements, resource capacity, and patient recruitment—sites can objectively quantify trial requirements and align them with available capabilities.

The framework synthesizes evidence from validated complexity assessment tools including OPAL [1], [5], PCT [3], TRACAT [4], and Pharm-CAT [13], alongside publicly available feasibility checklists [18]. It incorporates empirically-derived complexity dimensions, weighted scoring that reflects impact on trial success and site burden, and decision thresholds that map complexity scores to actionable recommendations.

Implementation of this checklist addresses a critical gap in clinical trial planning. Approximately one in four clinical trials fail to complete as planned, primarily due to inadequate feasibility assessment and poor recruitment [16], [18]. Structured complexity assessment has been shown to correlate with key performance indicators including time-to-site activation and participant enrollment [3], and can drive protocol simplification that reduces burden on sites and participants [3], [11]. Sites using structured feasibility management processes demonstrate improved efficiency, with median processing times of six days and clear documentation of decision rationale [19].

The checklist is designed to be practical and actionable. Sites can complete the assessment during the feasibility phase using readily available protocol information. The scoring system is objective and granular, with clear anchors for each item. Dimension-specific subtotals enable identification of specific areas of concern, while the total complexity score provides an overall assessment. The decision matrix integrates complexity scores with recruitment feasibility to generate clear go/no-go recommendations, with conditional pathways for trials requiring mitigation strategies.

Successful implementation requires local piloting and calibration. Sites should validate that complexity scores correlate with actual trial performance at their institution, adjust scoring criteria and decision thresholds to reflect site-specific context, and integrate the checklist into existing feasibility workflows. Continuous monitoring of predictive performance and refinement based on experience will enhance the tool's value over time.

By adopting this evidence-based complexity assessment framework, sites can improve trial selection decisions, optimize resource allocation, reduce startup delays, enhance enrollment success, and ultimately contribute to more efficient and successful clinical trial execution. The framework supports the fundamental goal of aligning trial opportunities with site capabilities, ensuring that accepted trials have realistic prospects for success while declining opportunities that would strain resources or face insurmountable barriers.
